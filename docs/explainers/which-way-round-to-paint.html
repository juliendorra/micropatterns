MicroPatterns · rendering architecture note

Which Way Round to Paint

The display list versus a streaming renderer. What each one can skip, what each one costs, and the measured numbers that decide between them — measured across twelve scripts — including the six recovered from the device itself.

The thing to understand first

The display list is not a buffer for convenience. It exists so the renderer can walk the scene backwards — front-most primitive first. Painting front-to-back is what makes it possible to skip work, and skipping work is the only thing the display list buys. Everything else about it is cost.

The mechanism

Two traversals, same picture

A MicroPatterns script is a sequence of drawing commands in painter's order: later commands cover earlier ones. There are two ways to arrive at the correct final image.

Back-to-front is the obvious one — execute commands in script order, let each overwrite whatever is under it. This needs no memory of the scene, so it can be done in a single streaming pass. It also means every overlapped pixel is written more than once, and the writes that get covered were wasted.

Front-to-back is the display list's trick. Collect every primitive first, then walk the list in reverse and paint the front-most thing first. Keep a bitmap of which pixels have been written this pass, and let the first writer win. Because you are going front to back, the first writer is by definition the one on top — so the picture is identical, and every covered pixel is never written at all.

Measured, and worth knowing

The Watchy genuinely fails to allocate the occupancy bitmap when the heap is fragmented, and initPixelOccupationMap() drops it and carries on. An earlier comment claimed that cost "speed, not correctness." It cost correctness: 15 of 15 corpus images differ. The renderer now detects the missing map and switches traversal direction, which is the correct handling — but it means the fallback path is a genuinely different renderer, not a slower one.

What each can skip

Three kinds of skipping, and who gets them

The renderer has three separate mechanisms for not doing work. They are usually discussed together, but only two of them depend on having the whole scene in hand — and the third, which is often the largest, does not.

The number you cannot get from the logs

207,648 skipped pixels sounds decisive, but the break-even is a ratio — skipped versus skipped-plus-drawn — and MicroPatternsDrawing counts the numerator only. There is no committed-pixel counter, deliberately: adding an increment to the innermost drawing loop would distort the measurement it exists to support (the harness notes reject exactly this, twice).

So the single most useful experiment here is not a rewrite. It is finding a way to count committed pixels that does not sit in the inner loop — a per-scanline span count would do it, since the span length is already known — and then reading the ratio straight off the existing logs.

The economics

When the bitmap pays for itself

Skipping a pixel is not free: you pay a bit-test on every pixel to save a draw on some of them. The rasterizer analysis puts the break-even at roughly cost(check + mark) / cost(full draw) ≈ 70 / 570 cycles ≈ 12%. Above that, the occupancy map wins.

The trap is that this threshold moves. The ranked rasterizer optimizations — folding scale into the inverse matrix, per-scanline interrupt checks, span rasterization — are all aimed at making the per-pixel draw cheaper. Make drawing 4× cheaper and the break-even rises to around 40%, because the test you pay on every pixel stays the same while the work you save shrinks.

Three things this changes

Occlusion culling is worth approximately nothing. Across twelve scripts, two canvases and 24 clock seeds, the largest number of items ever culled by occlusion was 15 — Seascape II at M5Paper size, out of 867 — and 9 at Watchy size. On the three city-family generators it was zero at every seed. One of the display list's two exclusive benefits does not show up in practice.

The occupancy map costs more than it saves — on the host. 23 of 24 configurations slower with it enabled, by up to 98%; the exception is a wash. This does not settle the device question, because the ESP32's per-pixel draw is far more expensive relative to the bit-test than the host's is. But it inverts the burden of proof: the rasterizer analysis called it "very likely a net win today", and that now needs measuring rather than assuming.

The real display lists are small. Peak across every clock seed, on the Watchy canvas: 480 bytes to 11,600 bytes, the top figure being Seascape II. The harness's city.mp, at 64,040 bytes, is 5.5× the largest real script and is the only thing in the corpus that makes the memory argument look urgent.

The trade

Pros and cons, stated plainly

Display list

Interpret fully, then rasterize in reverse

In favour
  • Occlusion culling. Whole primitives behind opaque ones are never rasterized at all.
  • Overdraw skipping. Covered pixels are never written. 207,648 of them on city.
  • A reusable intermediate. The same list can be rasterized more than once — paged output, or a second pass — without re-running the interpreter.
  • A clean phase split. Generation and rasterization are separately timed, separately optimized, separately interruptible.
  • Whole-scene knowledge. Anything needing lookahead — bounding all content, diffing against the previous frame, reordering by state — is possible only here.
Against
  • Memory grows with what the script draws. 40 bytes per primitive, and a REPEAT multiplies primitives without lengthening the script.
  • You allocate before you cull. city stored 20,737 items to discard 20,652.
  • The occupancy map is a second allocation that can fail on a fragmented heap — and its failure silently changes the picture.
  • The bit-test is paid on every pixel, including all the ones it never saves.

Streaming

Rasterize each primitive as the interpreter emits it

In favour
  • Memory is O(1) in primitives. The framebuffer is 5,000 bytes whether the script draws ten shapes or ten thousand.
  • Off-screen culling comes free and early — reject at emit time, before anything is stored.
  • No occupancy map, no snapshot pool, no vector growth. Three allocation sites and a fragmentation risk disappear.
  • No silent-degradation path. There is no optional structure whose absence changes output.
  • Simpler. One pass, one order, the order the script is written in.
Against
  • Every overlapped pixel is written more than once. Unbounded overdraw on layered scripts.
  • No occlusion culling at all. Fully hidden primitives are rasterized in full.
  • Nothing to re-use. A paged framebuffer means re-running the whole interpreter once per page.
  • No whole-scene analysis, now or later.
  • It does not exist yet — and it must stay pixel-identical to two other implementations.

The one that is easy to miss

Paged output multiplies streaming's cost

GxEPD2 renders through firstPage() / do {...} while (nextPage()). On the Watchy the page is full-height, so the body runs exactly once and this costs nothing. But paging is the standard way to trade CPU for framebuffer RAM — halve the page height, halve the buffer, run the body twice.

With a display list, that second pass is free of interpretation: you rasterize the same list again. With streaming, the display list is the interpretation, so there is nothing to replay — the whole script runs again, per page.

The call

When each one is the right answer

Display list, when the draw is expensive relative to a bit-test

This is the real condition, and it is about hardware, not scripts. A PSRAM-backed canvas reached through out-of-line per-pixel calls makes skipping worth a lot. A packed SRAM framebuffer written with a byte mask makes it worth little — which is why the host measurement came out negative and why the M5Paper is a better case for the display list than the Watchy.

Display list, when the script emits far more than it shows

Not for the reason usually given. Streaming culls off-screen primitives too — but only after the interpreter has computed their transformed bounds. If that computation is the expensive part, having a list lets the cull loop run over 40-byte PODs instead. city.mp's 20,652 off-screen items are the case.

Display list, when you will want the scene later

Paged output, re-rendering at a second scale, diffing against the previous frame for a partial refresh. None of these are possible once the primitives have been consumed. This is the benefit with no measurement against it, because nothing in the codebase uses it yet.

Streaming, when the real scripts are small

They are. Peak display list across 24 clock seeds on the Watchy canvas: 480 B to 11,600 B for every real script, Seascape II at the top. The structure being defended is holding at most eleven kilobytes — while Seascape II's parsed program is on the order of 146 KB, and is what actually reboots the watch on a button press.

Streaming, when the canvas is small

At 200 × 200 there are 40,000 pixels — 13× fewer than the M5Paper. Overdraw costs proportionally less, and the framebuffer write is a byte-mask on SRAM rather than a driver call, so what culling saves shrinks on both ends.

Streaming, when fragmentation is the failure mode

On a device that never reboots and has no PSRAM, removing growth-shaped allocations matters more than the bytes. Though note where the fragmentation actually comes from: the parse tree, not the display list. Streaming removes the smaller of the two.

What I would actually measure first

Two of the three questions are now answered, and the answers point the same way.

  • The occlusion cull count on real scripts. Answered: essentially zero. Twelve scripts, two canvases, 24 clock seeds; the high-water mark is 15 items out of 867, on Seascape II at M5Paper size. One of the display list's two exclusive benefits does not exist in practice.
  • Whether the occupancy map pays. Answered on the host: it does not, in 23 of 24 configurations, by up to 98%. Not answered on device, and that is the gap that matters — the ESP32's draw is far costlier relative to the check.
  • Still open: the same A/B on hardware. displaylist-nomap is byte-identical output, so this is a firmware flag and two serial logs, not a rewrite. If the device agrees with the host, the occupancy map, the occlusion buffer and the reverse-order traversal can all go — and once they are gone, the display list has no exclusive benefit left to defend and streaming follows almost for free.

That last point is the practical one. The order to settle this is not "build streaming and compare." It is: measure the map on device; if it loses, delete it; then the display list is just a buffer, and removing it is a small change rather than an architectural bet.
